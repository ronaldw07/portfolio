## Summary

Chora puts the small things you reach for all day into the space at the top of
your Mac: a master volume slider and one for each app, your day as a calendar
you can edit, a timer, and your music with album art, a live visualizer, and
synced lyrics. I lead it end to end: I decide what belongs in the notch, design
each tab, build it in Swift, and run the releases. It started from the
open-source boring.notch and has become its own product, with its own name,
website, roadmap, and update channel. It's free, and updates install
themselves.

![The notch expanded: a master volume slider and a separate slider for each app](/media/chora/volume.png)

## The problem

The notch is the most visible space on a MacBook, and most apps ignore it.
Meanwhile the controls I use constantly are scattered. Turning down one app
means turning down everything. Checking my next event means opening a
calendar. A timer means another app. Each one is small, but together they
break focus dozens of times a day.

## Who it's for

People who live on a MacBook with a notch and want small, frequent actions one
glance away: students, designers, and developers who juggle music, calls,
meetings, and focus sessions at once.

## What's already out there

- **Notch utilities like NotchNook**: cover media controls and shortcuts, but
  not per-app volume or a calendar you can edit.
- **macOS itself**: Control Center has one volume slider for the whole system,
  and no timeline you can drag around.

Chora's bet: the notch should be a place to do things, not just look at them.

## Design principles

Four design principles decide what gets built and what gets cut:

- **One glance, one action.** Everything fits in a short, wide space, so each
  tab does one thing and does it fast. If it needs a full window, it doesn't
  belong in the notch.
- **Control what you'd otherwise open an app for.** Per-app volume, dragging an
  event, undoing a reset timer.
- **Sharing space gracefully.** Music and a running timer sit in the notch
  together instead of fighting for it.
- **Stay out of the way.** It should be reliable enough to forget about, and
  updates should install without you thinking about them.

## Design decisions

### Two shapes for the same day

The notch is wide and short, so the calendar started as a horizontal timeline:
the whole day at a glance. But people read their day top to bottom, the way
phone and Google calendars show it, and a wide strip makes long days and
overlapping events hard to scan. Rather than pick one, the latest release adds
a top-to-bottom view behind a single button. Overlapping events sit side by
side, and pulling an event's bottom edge changes when it ends.

### Borrowing habits people already have

Most people who'd use a calendar in the notch already use Google Calendar, so
where Chora could either invent its own way or match Google's, it matches.
Events resize from the bottom edge only, as Google's do. The Today button is outlined, the views are Day, 4 Days, Week and
Month, and the keyboard shortcuts are Google's: T for today, J and K to move,
D, W and M for the views, C for a new event and / to search. Someone switching
over doesn't have to learn anything new.

### Calendars without another sign-in

The obvious move was a "Connect Google Calendar" button that loads events
straight from Google. I didn't build it. Anyone who already has Google on their
Mac would see every event twice, and every new user would hit Google's
"unverified app" warning and its 100-person cap just to see their own day.
Instead, events come from the accounts already on the Mac, so Google, iCloud
and Outlook all work with no sign-in at all, and an Add account button opens
the Mac's own setup screen. Signing in with Google is a single optional button
for the extras macOS can't do: answering invites, Meet links, event colors and
Google Tasks.

### Task times Google won't store

People asked to see their tasks at a time in the day, the way Google Calendar
shows them. Google's task service only shares a task's day with other apps, so
there was no time to import. Rather than leave tasks in a strip above the day,
Chora lets you give a task a time and keeps that time on the Mac. The tradeoff
is spelled out on the Add time button: Google Calendar won't see the time, but
the day in the notch finally shows everything you need to do and when.

### Weather without an account or a battery cost

Knowing it'll rain at 4 changes how you plan the day, so the calendar shows the
weather beside the date. Apple's own weather service needs a paid developer
account, so Chora uses Open-Meteo, which is free and needs no key. To keep it
light on battery and private, it asks macOS for a rough location (about a
kilometer), refreshes at most every 30 minutes, and only while the Calendar tab
is open. It is never polled in the background.

### Letting people choose the size

A top-to-bottom day needs height the notch doesn't have. Instead of a fixed
"big" size, you drag the notch's bottom edge to the height you want. The expand
button then becomes a shrink button, and expanding again returns to the size
you last chose, so the notch remembers your preference instead of making you
redo it.

### Typing instead of a form

A new-event form needs a title field, a date picker, start and end times, and a
calendar menu, which is a full window's worth of controls in a space the height
of a few lines. So adding an event is one text box: type "Lunch with Sam 1pm"
or "Gym tomorrow 6-7pm". The risk with plain language is guessing wrong, so a
line under the box shows exactly what will be created (title, day, times,
calendar) before you press Return, and Undo takes it back. A time with no day
goes on the day you're looking at, because that's the day you're thinking
about.

Typing is fast when you know the time; dragging is faster when you're looking
at a gap. So pressing on an empty part of the day and dragging draws the event
right where it goes, and a small box asks only for what the drag can't tell
you: a title, which calendar, a place, a note.

### Undo instead of dialogs

Dragging or deleting an event in a space this small invites mistakes, but a
confirmation dialog on every move would slow down every change to guard
against a rare one. So moves and resizes happen straight away, delete only
asks for a second tap on the same button, and every change can be undone, one
step at a time. In 0.9.4 that rule reached Google too: saving an edit closes the
box the moment you click, and changes sent to Google (a color, an invite answer,
a task ticked off or deleted) sit behind the same Undo. The tradeoff was waiting
on the network versus feeling instant, so the screen shows the change at once,
Google catches up in the background, and anything it refuses goes back with a
note. A deleted task stays only hidden until Undo is no longer offered, so it
can come back without being recreated.

## Heuristic review

I went through the calendar tab against Nielsen's usability heuristics, using
the real app. Two problems are still open, two are fixed, and several were
already covered.

### Still open

- **The Undo message hides behind the details box.** When an event's details
  are open, their dark backdrop sits over the Undo message, so the first click
  on Undo just closes the box. Undo replaced confirmation dialogs, so the
  safety net has to be reachable. *(User control and freedom.)*
- **The tab shortcuts are invisible.** Pressing 1 to 5 (or ⌥1 to ⌥5) switches
  tabs, but the numbers aren't shown anywhere in the app, so only people who
  read the release notes find them.
  Showing each tab's number when you hover over it would fix it.
  *(Recognition rather than recall; flexibility and efficiency.)*

### Fixed

- **The notch closed too early.** It closed too soon after the pointer left it.
  It now waits 2 seconds, or closes right away once the pointer is more than
  70 points away. *(User control and
  freedom.)*
- **The resize arrows flickered**, so it was hard to tell whether a drag would
  resize. They now stay steady.
  *(Visibility of system status.)*

### Already covered

- **Error prevention:** deleting asks for a second press, and changing a
  repeating event asks whether you mean this one or the whole series.
- **User control:** every change can be undone, and Escape cancels whatever
  you're in the middle of.
- **Helping people recover from errors:** when a save or delete fails, it says
  why in plain words: "This calendar may be read-only."

## What shipped

### Volume, per app

A master slider plus a slider for each app. Turn Discord down without touching
Spotify.

### A calendar you can edit

One day, 4 days, a week or a month, with the weather for each day. The day
runs as one wide timeline or top to bottom like a phone calendar. Drag events
to move them (across days in the week), pull the bottom edge to change the
end, click for details and a one-click Join for video calls, drag on an empty
spot or type a sentence to add an event, make it repeat on any schedule or
remind you, copy and paste, duplicate, edit or delete it, move it to another
calendar or recolor it, and undo any change (⌘Z too). A heads-up beside the closed notch says when an event starts in five
minutes. Search a year of events, answer Google invites, add guests and a Meet
link, and see and edit your Google Tasks on every day.

![Your day as a timeline in the notch, with events you can drag to move or resize](/media/chora/calendar.png)

### Timers that keep up

Lengths down to the second, a row of recent timers, a smooth progress ring,
and undo when you cancel by accident. In 0.10.0 the timer stopped being one at
a time: people run a few things at once, like the oven and a meeting, and a
single timer made them pick. The tab has no room for several rings, so one
ring shows the timer you're looking at while a narrow list beside it shows
every timer's time left, and the closed notch shows only the one ending
soonest, with "+1" for the rest. The stopwatch got laps, with the lap in
progress counting live under the total.

![The timer tab](/media/chora/timer.png)

### Music that shares the space

Album art, a live visualizer, and synced lyrics, now on by default. When a
timer is running, it sits next to the music instead of replacing it.

![The music tab with album art and a live visualizer](/media/chora/music.png)

### Quick to move around

Hover the notch and press 1 to 5 to switch tabs (or ⌥1 to ⌥5 from anywhere
while it's open), and the calendar has Google Calendar's own shortcuts. A
shelf holds files you drop on the notch, and clipboard history gets back
anything you copied.

## Getting it to people

Chora is a free download as a .dmg for Apple silicon Macs on macOS 14 or later.
macOS blocks apps it hasn't verified, and the first install used to need a
Terminal command, which looked suspicious to the people I most wanted to try it.
Now it's two clicks in System Settings ("Open Anyway"), with the steps right
under the download button so nobody gets stuck. After that, signed updates
install on their own. There's a Discord for setups and early versions, and a feedback form for
bugs and requests.

## Listening to users

The site has a public "Coming soon" list, and a "Your idea" box sends requests
straight onto it. The feedback form asks for what I need to act on a report:
steps to reproduce, the app and macOS version, and what you expected. I use
Chora every day, and the first things I built were the ones I kept missing.

## How I work

I run Chora like a small product team of one. Every change starts as a written
plan, gets tried on my own Mac, and ships only after it works. Releases are
small and frequent: 28 versions since September 2026, each with a
plain-language changelog entry that becomes the in-app update note, the GitHub
release, and the website's "What's new" at once. One release script builds,
signs, and publishes each version, so shipping a fix takes minutes. Chora runs
for days without quitting, so instead of waiting for a quit, the updater
installs a new version as soon as it downloads: a fix reaches everyone within
the hour.
