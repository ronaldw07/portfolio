## Summary

Approvals is a design concept, not a shipped product. It explores the kind of
request and approval flow most companies run on: someone needs a software
license, a piece of equipment, or travel, and the request passes through a
manager and sometimes finance before anything happens. The concept has three
parts: a one-sentence request with a preview, a decision card that gives an
approver everything they need on one screen, and a status page that always
says who has the request and what happens next. No one has used it yet, and
there are no results to report.

![The flow: request, manager, a spend-limit check, finance, done, and the paths back for questions and declines](/media/approvals/flow.png)

## The problem

Approval flows tend to fail in the same three places.

- **Asking is slow.** A long form asks for things the system already knows,
  like who your manager is and which cost center you belong to.
- **Deciding takes three tools.** The approver gets a link, then has to check
  the budget, the policy, and whether the team already has one somewhere else.
- **Waiting is blind.** The requester sees "Pending" with no name, no date, and
  no idea what comes next, so they chase it by email.

## Who it's for

Two people with opposite needs. The requester wants to ask once and stop
thinking about it. The approver gets many requests and wants to make a good
decision fast, without opening a spreadsheet for each one.

## Design principles

- **The system should fill in what it knows.** People should only describe
  what they need and why.
- **Show before sending.** A preview of exactly what goes out and who will see
  it makes a short input safe.
- **Every state names a person and a next step.** "With Finance since Tuesday"
  beats "Pending L2".
- **No dead ends.** A decline always carries a reason, and a question keeps the
  request's place in line.

## The design

### Asking in one sentence

The request is a single text box: "A yearly license for a diagramming tool,
$180, for the onboarding redesign." Underneath, a preview shows how it was
read: the item, the cost, who it goes to, and the reason. Any line can be
corrected before sending. This borrows the pattern I shipped in
[Chora](/work/chora), where one text box with a live preview replaced a
new-event form.

![A long request form beside a one-sentence request with a preview of what will be sent](/media/approvals/request.png)

### Deciding on one screen

The approver's card puts the requester's reason, the cost, the budget left,
the policy check, whether the team already owns one, and who's next on one
screen. The actions are Approve, Ask a question, and Decline, and Decline asks
for a reason that goes back with the request. Approvers who are away can hand
their queue to a delegate.

![The approver's queue and a decision card with cost, budget, policy, and the three actions](/media/approvals/approver.png)

### Knowing where it stands

The requester's page answers three questions in one line: who has it, since
when, and what happens next. Below it, a history keeps every question and
answer, so nobody has to search their inbox to understand a delay.

![The requester's status: a step tracker showing Finance as the current step, and a history of questions and approvals](/media/approvals/status.png)

## Open questions

These are the things I'd want to learn before building any of it:

- **Does one sentence hold up for big requests?** Travel or hiring may need
  more structure than a sentence and a preview can carry.
- **What do approvers actually check?** The decision card is my guess. Watching
  a few approvers work through a real queue would show which facts matter.
- **Where should it live?** Approvers may prefer to decide inside the chat or
  email they already use, with this page as the record.

## How I work

Mapped the flow first, including every way a request can come back, then
sketched the requester's and approver's screens as wireframes. Names and
amounts in the frames are sample data.
