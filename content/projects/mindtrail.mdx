## Summary

Mindtrail turns a big goal, like landing a design internship, into a map you
can see. Each goal is a journey. Each journey is a few colored paths, and each
path is a line of stops that ends at what you're aiming for. Beside the map, an
assistant helps you plan: it asks what you actually want, gives advice based on
what it knows about you, and changes the map as you talk, so you watch the plan
take shape. A Today view pulls the next steps, classes, and recruiting emails
into one clear day. It runs locally in the browser, with no account to create.

## The problem

Big goals are vague and long, and a to-do list hides their shape. A list can't
show that "apply to 10 companies" depends on "tailor my resume," or how far
along the whole thing is. Chat assistants give good advice, but it scrolls away
as text, and the plan itself lives nowhere. My own week was split the same way:
assignments in Canvas, recruiting emails in Gmail, events in Calendar, goals in
my head. I could never see the whole plan, or what to do next.

## Target user

Students and early-career people working toward a goal that takes months,
like a job search, a portfolio, or getting fit, while school and everything
else keeps going. They need a plan they can see at a glance, advice they can
trust, and an answer to what to do today.

## Competitors

- **Todoist and Apple Reminders**: fast capture and good check-off, but they
  show a flat list. Nothing shows the route to a goal or how far along you are.
- **Notion**: flexible enough to build a roadmap, which means you build it,
  then keep it up to date yourself.
- **ChatGPT and other chat assistants**: helpful in the moment, but the advice
  stays in the conversation. They can't see your plan, can't change it, and lose
  the thread between conversations.

Mindtrail's bet: the plan should be something you can see, and the assistant
should edit that plan directly instead of describing changes for you to make.

## Insight

Four design principles came out of using it every day:

- **Show the whole plan.** A goal should look like a route, with where you are,
  what's next, and where it ends.
- **The assistant works on the plan.** Advice should become a change on the map,
  not a paragraph to copy out.
- **Ask before assuming.** One good question beats generic advice.
- **Show where everything came from.** Anything created automatically shows its
  source.

## Solution

### A map of every goal

Each journey opens as a map. Its paths are colored lines of stops, in order,
ending at a flag for the goal. A filled circle with a check is done. A solid one
is accepted. A dashed one is a suggestion still waiting for your yes. The next
stop is tagged, so you can see where you are in a second. Click a stop to open
its page, tick the circle to finish it, or drag it along its line, or onto
another one, to reorder. A "Next up" list across all paths answers what to do
next.

![A journey as a map: colored paths of stops, the next stop on each tagged, with the assistant open beside it](/media/mindtrail/map.png)

### An assistant that plans with you

The assistant sits beside the map and works on it.

- **Asks first.** Before suggesting a plan, it asks one question with tappable
  answers drawn from your own notes.
- **Advises with context.** It knows your goals, what you've told it, and what
  you've uploaded, so advice fits you.
- **Changes the map.** Ask for a new path, a reordered step, or a daily goal and
  it appears on the map. Deletions wait for your yes.
- **Remembers.** Preferences you mention become saved facts you can see and
  edit, so it never asks twice.
- **Reads what you share.** Drop in a resume or paste a screenshot.
- **Adapts.** Record a result and it offers to rebuild the rest of the route
  around it.

![The assistant asking which kind of internship before suggesting daily goals](/media/mindtrail/assistant.png)

### Zooming into a step

Open any stop to see its own route of smaller steps and daily goals. Long routes
show the next three stops, with the rest one click away, and only the next stop
shows its full instructions. Dropdowns and date pickers became one plain-language
box: type *"measure my jump by Friday"* and the assistant fills in the title,
the how-to, what to record, and the due date.

![A step's route: the next three stops, the next one expanded, everything else one line](/media/mindtrail/step.png)

### Today, for doing

The map shows the whole plan. Today shows only what to do now. The first version
opened on a painting that filled the screen, a wall of widgets, and a featured
card that repeated the first to-do. Now the header is half the height and the
to-dos come right after it, grouped into Classes, Journeys, and Other. Star three
as today's focus and a live "2 of 3 done" count tracks them. Checking a to-do
crosses it off in place, and the trash can moves it to an archive where it can
be put back. Cluttered class titles like *W7M13: Design and Tech: Lighting [D10:
INTRO TO THEATRE F26]* became *Design and Tech: Lighting*, with the course
name underneath.

![Today: three starred to-dos up top, then Classes and Journeys, each a single clean line](/media/mindtrail/today.png)

### Showing the source

Job to-dos are created automatically from recruiting emails, so trust
matters. Each one names the company, flags when that application has closed,
and opens a card with a preview of the original email and a one-click link to
it in Gmail.

![A job to-do's card: the company, the application status, and the email it came from](/media/mindtrail/source-card.png)

### Designing for failure

Daily use surfaced the moments that break trust: a dropped connection that
froze the chat on "Thinking…", and emails that failed silently in the
background. Every request now ends in a clear state you can act on, the chat
keeps accepting messages while it works, and the app switches to a backup
model when one is unavailable. All motion is short and eased, and respects
reduced-motion settings.

## Distribution

Mindtrail runs locally as a browser app, with no server to host and no
account to create. It connects to Gmail, Google Calendar, and Canvas with your
own sign-in, and everything it learns stays on your machine.

## Adapting to user feedback

I was Mindtrail's first daily user, and the redesign above is a record of
that feedback: the page reload that made checked-off to-dos vanish, the route
that grew to fifteen stops, the suggestions that ignored what I'd already
said, and the job to-dos left over from applications that had already
closed. Each one became a design change I could verify against the same
screen that showed the problem.

## Process

Whenever something felt wrong, I captured the screen, redesigned it, and
checked the fix against that same screen, using my own classes, applications,
and goals rather than mock content. The screenshots on this page use sample
data. Underneath, memory search
was tuned against a 90-question benchmark split into a tuning set and a
held-out set, and it found the right past note for every held-out question.
Designed, built, and shipped solo, from problem framing to front end to back
end.
