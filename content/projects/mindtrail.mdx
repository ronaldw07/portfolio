## Summary

Mindtrail brings classes from Canvas, recruiting updates from Gmail, events
from Google Calendar, and long-term goals into a single Today view. A built-in
assistant breaks big goals into steps, remembers what you tell it, and keeps
the plan current as life changes. It runs locally in the browser, with no
account to create.

## The problem

My week lived in five places: assignments in Canvas, recruiting emails in
Gmail, events in Calendar, notes in one app, and goals in my head. Every tool
was a list. None of them answered the question that matters each morning:
*what should I do today?*

## Target user

Students and early-career people balancing school, a job search, and
personal goals at the same time. Their plans span months, but they get
decided one day at a time, usually in the five minutes before class.

## Competitors

- **Todoist and Apple Reminders**: fast capture and good check-off, but most
  tasks are typed in by hand, and nothing connects a to-do to the bigger goal
  it serves.
- **Notion**: flexible enough to build any planner, which means you have to
  build it, then keep it up to date yourself.
- **ChatGPT and other chat assistants**: helpful in the moment, but they don't
  know your deadlines, can't change your plan, and lose the thread between
  conversations.

Mindtrail's bet: the plan should assemble itself from the places your life
already happens, and the assistant should edit that plan directly instead of
describing changes for you to make.

## Insight

Four design principles came out of using it every day:

- **Today is for doing.** The first thing you see is what to do, not a
  dashboard. Nothing on Today waits on a model, so it opens instantly.
- **Never lose what someone typed.** Every error leaves a clear next step.
- **Show where everything came from.** Anything created automatically shows
  its source.
- **Ask before assuming.** One good question beats generic advice.

## Solution

### Rethinking Today

The first version opened on a painting that filled the screen, a wall of
widgets, and a featured card that repeated the first to-do. Checking off a task
reloaded the page and made it vanish.

Now the header is half the height and the to-dos come right after it, grouped
into Classes, Journeys, and Other. Star three
as today's focus and a live "2 of 3 done" count tracks them. Checking a to-do
crosses it off in place. The trash can moves it to an archive where it can
be put back, and a "Remove when done" switch suits people who prefer a clean
list. Cluttered class titles like *W7M13: Design and Tech: Lighting [D10:
INTRO TO THEATRE F26]* became *Design and Tech: Lighting*, with the course
name underneath.

![Today: three starred to-dos up top, then Classes and Journeys, each a single clean line](/media/mindtrail/today.png)

### Plans that stay readable

Journeys break big goals, like landing a summer internship, into steps and
smaller to-dos. Long routes show the next three stops with the rest one click
away, and only the next stop shows its full instructions. Dropdowns and date
pickers became one plain-language box: type *"measure my jump by Friday"* and
the assistant fills in the title, the how-to, what to record, and the due
date.

![A step's route: the next three stops, the next one expanded, everything else one line](/media/mindtrail/step.png)

### An assistant that knows you

- **Asks first.** Before suggesting a plan, it asks one question with
  tappable answers drawn from your own notes.
- **Remembers.** Preferences you mention become saved facts you can see and
  edit, so it never asks twice.
- **Reads what you share.** Drop in a resume or paste a screenshot.
- **Gets things done.** Changes happen as you ask, and deletions wait for
  your yes.
- **Adapts.** Record a result and it offers to rebuild the rest of the plan
  around it.

![The assistant asking which kind of internship before suggesting daily goals](/media/mindtrail/assistant.png)

### Showing the source

Job to-dos are created automatically from recruiting emails, so trust
matters. Each one names the company, flags when that application has closed,
and opens a card with a preview of the original email and a one-click link to
it in Gmail.

![A job to-do's card: the company, the application status, and the email it came from](/media/mindtrail/source-card.png)

### Designing for failure

Daily use surfaced the moments that break trust: a dropped connection that
froze the chat on "Thinking…", and emails that failed silently in the
background. Every request now ends in a clear state you can act on, the chat
keeps accepting messages while it works, and the app switches to a backup
model when one is unavailable.

### Motion and detail

Checking off, removing, and opening cards use short, eased transitions.
Replies settle in rather than pop, and all motion respects reduced-motion
settings.

## Distribution

Mindtrail runs locally as a browser app, with no server to host and no
account to create. It connects to Gmail, Google Calendar, and Canvas with your
own sign-in, and everything it learns stays on your machine.

## Adapting to user feedback

I was Mindtrail's first daily user, and the redesign above is a record of
that feedback: the page reload that made checked-off to-dos vanish, the route
that grew to fifteen stops, the suggestions that ignored what I'd already
said, and the job to-dos left over from applications that had already
closed. Each one became a design change I could verify against the same
screen that showed the problem.

## Process

Whenever something felt wrong, I captured the screen, redesigned it, and
checked the fix against that same screen, using my own classes, applications,
and goals rather than mock content. The screenshots on this page use sample
data. Underneath, memory search
was tuned against a 90-question benchmark split into a tuning set and a
held-out set, and it found the right past note for every held-out question.
Designed, built, and shipped solo, from problem framing to front end to back
end.
